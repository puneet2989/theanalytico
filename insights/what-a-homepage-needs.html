What a local business actually needs on a homepage

Most homepages try to say everything and end up saying nothing. Here is what actually earns its place above the fold, in order.

Published 14 September 2026

Open ten homepages for local businesses and you will see the same mistake nine times: a big photo, a vague tagline like "Quality Service You Can Trust", and no clear next step. None of it is wrong exactly. None of it does anything either.

A homepage has one job: tell a visitor within a few seconds that they are in the right place, and give them one obvious thing to do next. Everything on the page should serve that job or it is taking up room that something useful could be using.

Say what you do, plainly, first

The headline is not the place for personality. It is the place to answer the question the visitor actually has: what does this business do, and is it what I am looking for? "We build websites that bring businesses more customers" tells a visitor exactly what they are getting. "Digital solutions for the modern business" tells them nothing, and they will leave to find someone who will.

Say the service, say who it is for if that narrows anything useful, and leave the clever wordplay for later in the page, if at all.

Pick one action, not four

A homepage with a "Call now", a "Get a quote", a "Book online", and a newsletter signup is a homepage that has not decided what it wants the visitor to do. Every extra choice is a chance for someone to do nothing instead. Pick the single action that matters most for the business, usually a call, a form, or a booking, and make it the loudest thing on the page. Secondary actions can exist further down, quietly.

Real contact details, not a contact page you have to hunt for

A phone number and a way to reach the business should be visible without scrolling, not buried three clicks deep. For a local business specifically, a visitor is often trying to decide "can I trust this business enough to ring them", and a visible, real phone number answers that question before they have even read the rest of the page.

Load fast on a phone, not just on the office wifi

Most visits to a local business site happen on a phone, often with an ordinary mobile connection, not the fast wifi a site gets built and tested on. A homepage stacked with autoplaying video, a dozen fonts, and a slideshow feels fine on a laptop in the office and unusable on a phone in a car park. If a page has not finished loading by the time someone has decided whether to wait for it, the content on it is irrelevant.

Show the work, don't just claim it

"Trusted by businesses across Ireland" is a sentence, not evidence. A homepage is more convincing when it shows an actual example: a real project, a real photograph of finished work, a real client's site linked out. If a business genuinely does not have that yet, it is better to leave the section out than to fill it with a stock photograph of people in a meeting who have never worked there.

Cut before you add

Every section on a homepage should be there because it moves a visitor closer to contacting the business, not because it seemed like something a website "should" have. A mission statement nobody reads, a stats counter with numbers nobody can verify, three more paragraphs restating the headline in different words: none of it is helping. If you are not sure whether a section earns its place, take it out and see if anyone notices it is gone.
